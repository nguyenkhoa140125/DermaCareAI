Dermatology

Skin Conditions

Comprehensive clinical research from AAD, BAD, EADV and NICE guidelines.

⚠
All information is for educational research. Skin conditions require clinical examination by a dermatologist. Do not self-diagnose changing moles or suspicious lesions.

Quick Clinical Reference

Melanoma ABCDE

  • A — Asymmetry: one half unlike the other
  • B — Border: irregular, ragged, notched, blurred
  • C — Colour: multiple colours (tan/brown/black/red/white)
  • D — Diameter: over 6mm (can be smaller)
  • E — Evolution: any change in size, shape, colour, bleeding
  • ANY suspicious or changing mole: urgent dermatology referral

Eczema & Psoriasis

  • Emollients 250g+/week: cornerstone of all eczema management
  • TCS: match potency to site (mild face, moderate body, potent thick plaques)
  • Dupilumab IL-4/IL-13: highly effective moderate-severe AD from age 6mo
  • IL-17 inhibitors: fastest psoriasis clearance (PASI 90+ in 60-70%)
  • Biologic step-up: PASI over 10, DLQI over 10, or BSA over 10%

Acne Treatment Steps

  • Topical retinoid + BPO first-line (never antibiotics alone)
  • Oral antibiotics max 3 months, always with BPO to prevent resistance
  • Isotretinoin for severe/nodulocystic/scarring acne
  • PIH in darker skin: retinoids + azelaic acid + strict SPF 50+
  • Hormonal acne females: COCP, spironolactone

Contact Dermatitis

  • Patch testing: gold-standard for allergic contact dermatitis
  • Common allergens: nickel, fragrances, preservatives (MI/MCI), PPD
  • ICD: most common occupational skin disease
  • Management: strict allergen avoidance + TCS for active inflammation
Common Skin Condition

Acne Vulgaris

Research on acne pathogenesis, grading, treatment ladder, isotretinoin, and scarring/PIH prevention from AAD, BAD and NICE guidelines.

Acne Pathogenesis and Grading

Four key pathogenic factors: excess sebum (androgen-driven DHT via 5-alpha reductase), follicular hyperkeratinisation (comedone formation), Cutibacterium acnes colonisation and biofilm, and inflammation (innate + adaptive immune responses).

Lesion Types

  • Non-inflammatory: Open comedones (blackheads — oxidised melanin, not dirt), closed comedones (whiteheads)
  • Inflammatory: Papules (red raised), pustules (pus-filled), nodules (deep solid over 5mm), cysts (deep fluctuant, highest scarring risk)

Grading

  • Mild: Mainly comedones, few papules/pustules, no nodules
  • Moderate: Multiple papules/pustules, few nodules, face +/- trunk
  • Severe: Extensive papules/pustules, multiple nodules/cysts, high scarring risk

Acne in Skin of Colour (Fitzpatrick IV-VI)

PIH is often more distressing than the acne itself. Early treatment prevents PIH. Avoid aggressive irritating treatments. Azelaic acid, niacinamide, and vitamin C are particularly useful. Laser/light therapies require modified settings to avoid paradoxical PIH worsening.

Acne Treatment Ladder

Step 1 — Topical (Mild)

  • Benzoyl peroxide (BPO) 2.5-10%: Bactericidal, anti-inflammatory, reduces resistance. Start 2.5%, increase gradually. Bleaches fabrics — warn patients.
  • Topical retinoids (adapalene 0.1-0.3%, tretinoin 0.025-0.1%): First-line for comedonal acne. Apply pea-size at night, start 2-3x/week, increase as tolerated. Retinoid dermatitis common initially.
  • Topical antibiotics (clindamycin 1%): ALWAYS combine with BPO — never monotherapy (resistance). Apply twice daily.
  • Azelaic acid 15-20%: Anti-inflammatory, anti-comedogenic, reduces PIH. Safe in pregnancy. Well-tolerated in darker skin types.
  • Fixed combinations (adapalene+BPO, clindamycin+BPO): Improve compliance, reduce resistance.

Step 2 — Systemic (Moderate)

  • Oral doxycycline 100mg OD/BD: Maximum 3 months. Always add topical BPO concurrently. Avoid with isotretinoin (raised ICP risk).
  • Combined oral contraceptive pill (females): Co-cyprindiol (Dianette), ethinylestradiol+norgestimate. For hormonal/late-onset acne. VTE risk counselling.
  • Spironolactone 25-200mg OD (females only): Anti-androgen, off-label UK. Monitor potassium and blood pressure.

Step 3 — Oral Isotretinoin

See Isotretinoin tab for full details. For severe/nodulocystic/scarring acne or moderate acne failing adequate trials of topical and systemic treatments.

Oral Isotretinoin (Roaccutane)

Most effective acne treatment available — targets all four pathogenic factors. Reduces sebum production up to 80%.

Dosing

  • Typical dose: 0.5-1mg/kg/day; cumulative target 120-150mg/kg for lasting remission
  • Start low (0.25-0.5mg/kg) to minimise initial flare, increase over 4-8 weeks
  • Course: typically 4-9 months

Monitoring (mandatory)

  • LFTs and fasting lipids: baseline, 4-6 weeks, then every 1-3 months
  • FBC: baseline; mood assessment at every visit

TERATOGENICITY — Pregnancy Prevention Programme

Highly teratogenic — ABSOLUTE CONTRAINDICATION in pregnancy. In UK, all patients registered on IPPA (Isotretinoin Patient and Prescriber Access) system. Females: two effective contraception forms mandatory 1 month before, during, and 1 month after. Monthly pregnancy tests.

Common Side Effects

  • Dry lips/skin/eyes (universal — lip balm, emollients, lubricating eye drops essential)
  • Elevated triglycerides and cholesterol, transient LFT elevation
  • Musculoskeletal: myalgia, arthralgia (especially with exercise)
  • Photosensitivity: SPF 50+ daily mandatory
  • Mood changes: controversial link — discuss with patient and family, report concerns

Acne Scarring and PIH

Scar Types

  • Icepick: Narrow, deep, sharply defined. Punch excision/elevation.
  • Boxcar: Broad base, well-defined vertical edges. Subcision + filler, fractional laser.
  • Rolling: Sloping edges, wave-like. Subcision most effective.
  • Hypertrophic/keloidal: Raised. Intralesional steroids, silicone, PDL laser.

Treatment Options

  • Fractional laser (ablative CO2/non-ablative): most effective overall, caution in dark skin (PIH risk)
  • Microneedling/RFMN: good evidence, lower PIH risk in darker skin
  • Chemical peels (glycolic, salicylic, TCA): superficial to medium depth
  • Subcision: rolling scars, breaks fibrotic bands
  • Punch excision or elevation: icepick scars
  • Dermal fillers (HA): temporary volume for rolling/boxcar

Post-Inflammatory Hyperpigmentation (PIH)

Not true scarring. Persists months-years. Essential: treat active acne + strict daily SPF 50+ (most important intervention). Topical retinoids, azelaic acid 15-20%, niacinamide 5-10%, vitamin C serum, tranexamic acid (topical or oral 250mg BD), hydroquinone 2-4% (short courses). Avoid aggressive peels/lasers without expertise in darker skin types (Fitzpatrick IV-VI).

Chronic Inflammatory

Psoriasis

Research on psoriasis types, severity assessment, topical/systemic/biologic treatments, and psoriatic arthritis from BAD, AAD, EADV and NICE guidelines.

Psoriasis Overview and Clinical Types

Chronic immune-mediated inflammatory condition. 2-3% of global population. Accelerated keratinocyte proliferation (28-day cycle reduced to 3-4 days). Strong genetic component (HLA-Cw6). Systemic disease: psoriatic arthritis (25-30%), cardiovascular disease, metabolic syndrome, IBD, depression.

Clinical Types

  • Plaque psoriasis (~85%): Well-demarcated erythematous plaques with thick silvery scale. Elbows, knees, scalp, lower back. Auspitz sign (pinpoint bleeding on scale removal). Koebner phenomenon (lesions at trauma sites).
  • Guttate: Small teardrop lesions on trunk/limbs. Triggered by streptococcal throat infection 2-3 weeks prior. Common in young adults/children. Often spontaneously resolves.
  • Inverse/flexural: Skin folds (axillae, groin, submammary). Bright red, well-demarcated, minimal scale. Commonly misdiagnosed as fungal/intertrigo.
  • Pustular generalised (von Zumbusch): EMERGENCY with fever and systemic toxicity. Sterile pustules.
  • Erythrodermic: 90%+ BSA. EMERGENCY — temperature dysregulation, cardiac failure.
  • Nail psoriasis: Pitting (pathognomonic), onycholysis, subungual hyperkeratosis, oil-drop sign. Affects 50% of patients. Strongly predicts psoriatic arthritis.

Severity Assessment (PASI and DLQI)

  • PASI 75: Traditional benchmark (75% improvement from baseline). Minimum response threshold for older biologics.
  • PASI 90: Modern standard for biologic era (90% improvement). Most current trials use this as primary endpoint.
  • PASI 100 (complete clearance): Achievable with IL-17 inhibitors (25-50%) and IL-23 inhibitors (20-40%).
  • Moderate-severe criteria (NICE/BAD): Any one of: BSA over 10%, PASI over 10, DLQI over 10. Difficult sites (face, hands, feet, genitalia, nails, scalp) also considered even if below threshold.
  • DLQI (Dermatology Life Quality Index): 10-question QoL score 0-30. DLQI over 10 = very large effect on quality of life. Required alongside PASI for biologic approval in most countries.

Topical Treatments and Phototherapy

Topical First-Line

  • Calcipotriol/vitamin D analogues: Reduce keratinocyte proliferation, anti-inflammatory, no skin atrophy. Max 100g/week (hypercalcaemia risk). Irritating on face/flexures.
  • Topical corticosteroids: Effective for flares. Potent (betamethasone 0.1%) for body plaques; mild for face/flexures. Risk of atrophy, tachyphylaxis with overuse.
  • Combination calcipotriol+betamethasone (Enstilar foam/Dovobet gel/Xamiol scalp): Convenient once-daily, additive efficacy, widely used first-line for moderate plaque. Foam formulation excellent for scalp.
  • Coal tar: Anti-proliferative, antipruritic. Messy but effective, especially scalp shampoos and chronic plaques.
  • Dithranol (short-contact therapy): 0.1-3%, applied 20-30 min then washed off. Effective for thick plaques. Stains skin/clothes.

Phototherapy

  • Narrowband UVB (NB-UVB, 311-313nm): Most-used phototherapy. 3x/week clinic. Response 60-70%. Safe in pregnancy. Lower carcinogenicity than PUVA.
  • PUVA (psoralen + UVA): More effective for thick plaques, palmoplantar disease. Higher SCC risk with cumulative doses. Maximum lifetime sessions. Less used now.
  • Excimer laser (308nm): Targeted for localised plaques, palmoplantar psoriasis.

Systemic and Biologic Treatments

Conventional Systemic

  • Methotrexate 7.5-25mg once weekly: Folic acid 5mg once weekly (not same day) mandatory. Monitor FBC/LFTs every 2-3 months. Hepatotoxicity risk (cumulative). TERATOGENIC.
  • Ciclosporin 2.5-5mg/kg/day: Rapid onset, short-term rescue. Monitor BP and renal function closely. Not for long-term continuous use.
  • Acitretin 10-50mg daily: Pustular and erythrodermic psoriasis. HIGHLY TERATOGENIC — females must avoid pregnancy during AND 3 years after stopping.

Biologics

  • Anti-TNF (adalimumab, etanercept, infliximab): First approved biologics. Screen TB (IGRA) and hepatitis B/C before starting. Biosimilars available (cost reduction).
  • Anti-IL-12/23 (ustekinumab): SC every 12 weeks after loading. Good safety, convenient. Effective for skin and psoriatic arthritis.
  • Anti-IL-17 (secukinumab, ixekizumab, bimekizumab): Fastest and highest skin clearance. PASI 90 in 60-70%+, PASI 100 in 30-50%. Avoid in active IBD. Oral candidiasis risk.
  • Anti-IL-23 (guselkumab, risankizumab, tildrakizumab): Quarterly dosing, durable remission, excellent safety. Drug-free remission possible with risankizumab in some patients.
  • TYK2 inhibitor (deucravacitinib, oral): Selective, oral. Favourable safety vs pan-JAK inhibitors. Does NOT carry the same class-level VTE/CV/malignancy warnings.

Psoriatic Arthritis (PsA)

Inflammatory arthropathy in 25-30% of psoriasis patients. May precede skin disease. Seronegative (RF negative). Manage jointly with rheumatology.

Clinical Patterns

  • Distal interphalangeal (DIP) joint predominant — with nail changes
  • Asymmetric oligoarthritis (under 5 joints)
  • Symmetric polyarthritis (RA-like)
  • Spondyloarthritis (axial, sacroiliitis, inflammatory back pain)
  • Arthritis mutilans (destructive, telescoping digits — rare)

Hallmarks

  • Dactylitis (sausage digit): Diffuse swelling of entire digit (all 3 joints + tenosynovitis)
  • Enthesitis: Achilles tendon, plantar fascia, patellar tendon insertion sites

Treatment

NSAIDs for mild. DMARDs (methotrexate, sulfasalazine, leflunomide) for peripheral joints. Biologics treat BOTH skin and joints: anti-TNF (first-line biologic), IL-17 inhibitors, IL-23 inhibitors, JAK inhibitors (upadacitinib, tofacitinib) particularly effective for enthesitis and dactylitis.

Dermato-Oncology

Skin Cancer

Research on melanoma, BCC, SCC, actinic keratosis, dermoscopy and ABCDE criteria from AAD, BAD and NICE guidelines.

⚠
Any changing mole, new pigmented lesion, non-healing sore, or suspicious skin growth: urgent dermatology assessment. AI cannot substitute for dermoscopy and clinical examination by a dermatologist.

Melanoma

Most dangerous skin cancer. Early detection critical — 5-year survival 98% (stage I) vs ~25% (stage IV).

ABCDE Clinical Signs

  • A — Asymmetry: one half unlike the other
  • B — Border: irregular, ragged, notched, blurred
  • C — Colour: multiple colours (tan/brown/black/red/white/blue)
  • D — Diameter: over 6mm (can be smaller)
  • E — Evolution: ANY change in size, shape, colour; new bleeding/crusting/itching

Subtypes

  • Superficial spreading (~70%): Slow radial growth before vertical invasion. Most common.
  • Nodular (~15%): Rapid vertical growth from onset. Most aggressive.
  • Lentigo maligna melanoma: Elderly, chronically sun-damaged skin. Slow growing.
  • Acral lentiginous: Palms/soles/subungual. Most common subtype in Asian and African-heritage populations. NOT sun-related.

Treatment

  • Wide local excision: 1cm margins for in situ/Breslow under 1mm; 1-2cm for thicker
  • SLNB for Breslow over 0.8mm or high-risk features
  • Adjuvant: pembrolizumab/nivolumab (PD-1) or dabrafenib+trametinib (BRAF V600E mutant)
  • Advanced: PD-1/CTLA-4 combination (nivolumab+ipilimumab) or BRAF+MEK inhibitors

Basal Cell Carcinoma (BCC)

Most common skin cancer globally (~80% NMSC). Slow growing, locally invasive, rarely metastasises. Excellent prognosis if treated early.

Clinical Types

  • Nodular (most common): Pearly papule/nodule with rolled border, telangiectasiae. May ulcerate (rodent ulcer).
  • Superficial: Erythematous scaly patch on trunk. May resemble eczema.
  • Morphoeic/sclerosing: Scar-like, ill-defined. Most aggressive, highest recurrence. Requires Mohs surgery.
  • Pigmented: Blue-black, more common in darker skin. May mimic melanoma.

Treatment

  • Surgical excision (gold-standard): 3-5mm margins depending on subtype/site
  • Mohs micrographic surgery: high-risk/facial sites, recurrent BCC, Gorlin syndrome
  • Topical (superficial BCC only): imiquimod 5% cream 5x/week x6 weeks, or PDT
  • Radiotherapy: elderly/inoperable
  • Hedgehog inhibitors (vismodegib/sonidegib): advanced/metastatic or Gorlin syndrome

SCC and Actinic Keratosis

Actinic Keratosis (AK) — Pre-malignant

Rough, scaly, erythematous papules/patches on sun-damaged skin. Individual lesion SCC progression risk ~0.1-0.5%/year. Field treatment preferred:

  • 5-fluorouracil cream (Efudix 5%): Apply BD for 3-4 weeks. Intense inflammatory reaction expected (treatment working). Highly effective.
  • Imiquimod 5%: Immune-stimulant, 3x/week for 4 weeks. Less intense reaction than 5-FU.
  • Photodynamic therapy (PDT): Best for thick/hyperkeratotic AKs. Cosmetically excellent. Daylight PDT available.
  • Diclofenac 3% gel: Mild, for sensitive/elderly patients, 60-90 days.
  • Cryotherapy: Individual lesions; does not treat subclinical field disease.

Squamous Cell Carcinoma (SCC)

  • Second most common skin cancer. Metastatic potential ~5% (higher in immunosuppressed)
  • High-risk: over 2cm, over 2mm depth, poor differentiation, perineural invasion, immunosuppressed, lip/ear site
  • Treatment: excision (4mm minimum margins), Mohs for high-risk/facial sites, SLNB, cemiplimab/pembrolizumab for advanced/metastatic
  • Transplant recipients: 65-250x increased SCC risk — 3-6 monthly skin surveillance

Dermoscopy

Handheld polarised-light dermoscope visualises subsurface structures, dramatically improving diagnostic accuracy. Reduces unnecessary excision of benign lesions while improving early melanoma detection.

Two-Step Algorithm

  • Step 1: Melanocytic vs non-melanocytic lesion? Look for pigment network, globules, homogeneous blue. If non-melanocytic: identify specific pattern (SK, DF, BCC, angioma).
  • Step 2: If melanocytic: benign or malignant? Pattern analysis, ABCD dermoscopic rule, or 7-point checklist.

Dermoscopic Red Flags for Melanoma

  • Atypical pigment network (irregular meshwork)
  • Blue-white veil (irregular diffuse blue-white haze)
  • Atypical vascular patterns (irregular vessels, milky red areas)
  • Regression structures (white scar-like areas, peppering/granularity)
  • Irregular streaks, pseudopods, radial streaming at periphery

Skin Cancer in Darker Skin Types (Fitzpatrick IV-VI)

  • Acral lentiginous melanoma: Most common melanoma subtype in Asian/African-heritage populations. Palms, soles, subungual (subungual = under nails). NOT UV-related. Hutchinson sign (periungual pigmentation) = urgent referral. Often diagnosed late.
  • SCC in dark skin: Chronic wounds/scars (Marjolin ulcer), areas of chronic inflammation (discoid lupus scars), HPV-related. Less UV-related than in Fitzpatrick I-II.
  • Pigmented BCC: More common in darker skin types, often mimics melanoma clinically.
  • Kaposi sarcoma: Violaceous plaques/nodules. Common in sub-Saharan Africa, HIV-positive patients.
  • Sun protection still important: SPF 30+ for all skin types — prevents photoageing, melasma, lupus exacerbation, and polymorphous light eruption.

JAK Inhibitors in Dermatology

Oral JAK Inhibitors

  • Baricitinib (Olumiant) 4mg OD — JAK1/2: Licensed for moderate-severe atopic eczema AND severe alopecia areata (BRAVE-AA trials). Rapid onset. JAK class warnings: VTE, cardiovascular events, malignancy — mainly concern from RA data. Caution over-65s, smokers, CV risk.
  • Upadacitinib (Rinvoq) 15/30mg OD — JAK1 selective: Licensed for atopic eczema (highest efficacy oral option), PsA, axSpA, RA, IBD. Acne-like rash is specific side effect. JAK class warnings apply.
  • Abrocitinib (Cibinqo) 100/200mg OD — JAK1 selective: Licensed for moderate-severe atopic eczema. Nausea common initially. Platelet monitoring initially. JAK class warnings.
  • Ritlecitinib (Litfulo) 50mg OD — JAK3/TEC: Licensed for severe alopecia areata from age 12+. Well-tolerated. Selective mechanism.
  • Deucravacitinib (Sotyktu) 6mg OD — TYK2 selective: Oral, licensed for moderate-severe plaque psoriasis. Allosteric TYK2 inhibitor — does NOT carry pan-JAK class warnings for VTE/CV/malignancy. More favourable monitoring requirements.

Topical JAK Inhibitor

  • Ruxolitinib 1.5% cream (Opzelura) — JAK1/2: FDA/EMA approved for non-segmental vitiligo (most effective repigmentation) and atopic eczema as steroid alternative. Apply BD. Minimal systemic absorption.

Phototherapy

Narrowband UVB (NB-UVB, 311-313nm)

Most widely used phototherapy. 3x/week clinic. Indications: psoriasis, atopic eczema, vitiligo, mycosis fungoides, pityriasis lichenoides, PLE desensitisation. Safe in pregnancy. Lower carcinogenicity than PUVA. Response: psoriasis 60-70%, vitiligo best on face/proximal limbs.

PUVA (Psoralen + UVA)

Oral 8-MOP or topical psoralen + UVA (320-400nm). More effective than NB-UVB for thick/palmoplantar plaques. Maximum lifetime dose limitations (SCC risk). UV-protective glasses on treatment days.

Photodynamic Therapy (PDT)

Topical photosensitiser (ALA or methyl-ALA) applied then activated by visible light. Preferentially accumulates in rapidly proliferating cells. Indications: actinic keratoses (excellent, cosmetically superior to cryotherapy for field treatment), superficial BCC, Bowen's disease, acne. Conventional PDT: highly effective but 24-48h photosensitivity. Daylight PDT: lower pain profile, suitable for mild-moderate AKs.

Dermatology Procedures

  • Cryotherapy (liquid nitrogen, -196°C): Warts, AKs, seborrhoeic keratoses, molluscum. Quick, no anaesthetic. Risk of hypopigmentation in dark skin (Fitzpatrick IV-VI) — use cautiously.
  • Curettage & cautery: BCC, SCC, seborrhoeic keratoses, warts.
  • Excision biopsy: Suspicious lesions, skin tumours. Punch biopsy for inflammatory conditions. Shave biopsy for superficial lesions (never for suspected melanoma).
  • Intralesional steroids: Triamcinolone acetonide 5-40mg/mL. Keloids, hypertrophic scars, alopecia areata, nodular prurigo, cystic acne, nail psoriasis.
  • Mohs micrographic surgery: High-risk skin cancers (BCC/SCC) on face. Staged excision with 100% margin examination. Highest cure rates.
  • Laser treatments: PDL for vascular/rosacea telangiectasia/port wine stains; Q-switched/picosecond Nd:YAG for tattoo removal/pigmentation; fractional CO2/Er:YAG for acne scarring/photoageing; excimer 308nm for vitiligo/psoriasis.
  • Chemical peels: Superficial (glycolic/salicylic): acne/PIH; medium (TCA 35%): AKs/scarring. Deep phenol rarely used. Caution in Fitzpatrick IV-VI (PIH risk).
  • Patch testing: Gold-standard for allergic contact dermatitis. European standard battery + supplementary panels. Allergens applied to back, readings at 48h and 96h (+/- 7 days).

Sun Protection Science

  • SPF ratings: SPF 30 blocks ~97% UVB; SPF 50 blocks ~98%. Broad-spectrum products protect against UVA too (look for PPD rating, PA+++, or UVA circle logo).
  • Application amount: 1/4 teaspoon (~1.5g) for face and neck. Apply 30 minutes before exposure. Reapply every 2 hours and after swimming/sweating. Most people apply only 25-50% of required amount.
  • Physical vs chemical sunscreens: Mineral (zinc oxide, titanium dioxide): reflect UV, immediate protection, less irritating, preferred for sensitive skin, rosacea, acne, darker skin tones. Chemical: absorb UV, cosmetically elegant but may irritate. Modern tinted mineral formulations avoid white cast.
  • All skin types need SPF: For all Fitzpatrick types — prevents melasma, PIH, photoageing, lupus exacerbation, polymorphous light eruption, and reduces skin cancer risk.
  • Vitamin D: Concerns about sunscreen blocking vitamin D production are largely theoretical at typical real-world use levels. Supplement rather than risk skin cancer through UV exposure.

Trichology & Nail Disorders

Hair & Nail Disorders

Research on alopecia, hair loss investigations, nail diseases, and treatment options from BAD, AAD and IADVL guidelines.

Alopecia Areata (AA)

Autoimmune T-cell mediated attack on anagen follicles. Sharply demarcated round/oval patches of non-scarring hair loss. Exclamation mark hairs (tapered distally) are characteristic. Spontaneous regrowth in ~50% within 1 year for limited disease, but recurrence common.

Classification

  • AA (patchy): one or more patches, scalp most common
  • Alopecia totalis (AT): complete scalp hair loss
  • Alopecia universalis (AU): complete scalp + body hair loss
  • Ophiasis: band-like at margins — poor prognosis for regrowth

Treatment

  • Limited (<50%): Intralesional triamcinolone acetonide 5-10mg/mL (most effective for localised AA, 6-8 weekly), topical potent steroids, topical minoxidil adjunct
  • Extensive/AT/AU: Contact immunotherapy (DPCP/SADBE, weekly sensitisation, 30-60% response rates); systemic steroids for rapid-onset; PUVA
  • Severe AA — JAK inhibitors (major 2022-2024 advance): Baricitinib 4mg OD (JAK1/2, BRAVE-AA trials, NICE approved), ritlecitinib 50mg OD (JAK3/TEC, approved from age 12+). Significant regrowth in ~35-40% at 36 weeks.

Androgenetic Alopecia (AGA)

Most common hair loss in males and females. DHT-mediated follicle miniaturisation. Gradual, progressive, non-scarring. Male pattern (Hamilton-Norwood: frontotemporal + vertex thinning). Female pattern (Ludwig: diffuse crown thinning, frontal hairline preserved).

Evidence-Based Treatments

  • Minoxidil topical (2% females, 5% males, OD-BD): Prolongs anagen phase. FDA-approved. Requires ongoing use (shedding resumes on stopping). Initial shedding for 2-3 months expected.
  • Oral minoxidil (0.625-2.5mg females, 2.5-5mg males): Growing evidence, effective at low doses. Monitor for fluid retention, hypertrichosis.
  • Finasteride 1mg OD (males only): 5-alpha reductase inhibitor, reduces DHT ~70%. TERATOGENIC (females of childbearing age must not handle crushed tablets). Sexual dysfunction ~2-4% (reversible on stopping in most).
  • Dutasteride 0.5mg OD (males, off-label AGA): Dual type I+II 5-alpha reductase inhibitor, more potent DHT reduction.
  • FUE/FUT hair transplantation: Permanent. Results at 12-18 months. Requires adequate donor hair.
  • LLLT (low-level laser therapy): FDA-cleared adjunct. Modest evidence.

Telogen Effluvium (TE)

Diffuse shedding 2-3 months after a trigger (telogen phase lag). Most common cause of diffuse hair loss in adult women. Usually self-limiting, regrowth in 3-6 months after treating the cause.

Common Triggers

  • High fever/severe illness (including COVID-19)
  • Major surgery or significant haemorrhage
  • Childbirth (postpartum TE — very common, self-limiting)
  • Crash dieting/nutritional deficiency (iron, ferritin, protein, zinc, vitamin D)
  • Thyroid dysfunction (hypo- or hyperthyroidism)
  • Emotional stress, significant life events
  • Medications: anticoagulants, retinoids, beta-blockers, lithium, hormonal changes

Investigation

  • FBC, ferritin (AIM over 70 mcg/L for optimal hair growth), TFTs
  • Vitamin D, zinc, B12, folate, LFTs, hormonal profile (if indicated)

Management

Treat underlying cause. Nutritional optimisation especially iron/ferritin. Reassurance: shedding is temporary. Topical minoxidil speeds recovery. Chronic TE (>6 months) needs more thorough investigation.

Nail Disorders

Onychomycosis

Most common nail disorder, ~10% general population. Confirm with nail clippings for microscopy + culture BEFORE starting treatment. Oral terbinafine 250mg OD: 3 months toenails, 6 weeks fingernails (most effective). Itraconazole pulse therapy alternative. Topical amorolfine 5% lacquer for single nail/superficial infection or adjunct.

Nail Psoriasis

Pitting (pathognomonic), onycholysis, subungual hyperkeratosis, oil-drop sign. Strongly predicts psoriatic arthritis. Biologics (IL-17, IL-23 inhibitors) most effective systemic treatment. Intralesional triamcinolone into proximal nail fold for limited disease.

Paronychia

  • Acute: S. aureus, post-trauma/nail-biting. Warm soaks, topical antibiotics (mild), oral flucloxacillin (spreading), I&D for fluctuant abscess.
  • Chronic: Candida + irritant exposure. Keep dry, barrier protection, topical antifungal+steroid. Common in healthcare workers, hairdressers, cooks.

Subungual Pigmentation

Hutchinson sign (periungual extension onto proximal/lateral nail folds) = strongly suggests acral lentiginous melanoma. Any suspicious subungual pigmentation without clear trauma history: URGENT dermatology assessment.

Dermatological Emergencies

🚨
SKIN EMERGENCIES — 112 (India) / 999 (UK) / 911 (US): Anaphylaxis (urticaria + airway symptoms/collapse), SJS/TEN (skin peeling + fever + mucosal involvement), Necrotising fasciitis (spreading red/dusky skin + severe pain + fever), Erythroderma (90%+ BSA + systemic symptoms). ALL LIFE-THREATENING.

🚨 Anaphylaxis

Recognition

Urticaria/angioedema + respiratory symptoms (wheeze, stridor) and/or cardiovascular collapse following allergen exposure (foods, drugs, stings, latex).

Immediate Actions

  • IM adrenaline 0.5mg adult (mid-outer thigh) — FIRST and most important treatment
  • Call 112/999/911 IMMEDIATELY
  • Lay flat, legs raised (sit up if breathing difficulty)
  • Oxygen; IV access; IV fluids
  • IM chlorphenamine 10mg + IV hydrocortisone 200mg (secondary — NOT instead of adrenaline)
  • Second adrenaline dose after 5-15 min if no improvement
  • All at-risk patients: carry TWO adrenaline auto-injectors (EpiPen/Jext) at all times

🚨 SJS / TEN

Recognition

Target lesions or blistering rash on trunk + skin detachment (Nikolsky sign positive) + fever + mucosal involvement (mouth/eyes/genitalia). Drug-induced in most cases (allopurinol, sulfonamides, anticonvulsants, NSAIDs, nevirapine).

Critical Actions

  • HOSPITAL IMMEDIATELY — burns unit/ICU for TEN (>30% BSA)
  • STOP causative drug immediately — single most important intervention
  • Supportive: IV fluids, nutrition, temperature regulation, wound care
  • Ophthalmology review (can cause blindness)
  • SCORTEN scoring for mortality prediction
  • Mortality: SJS ~10%, TEN 30-40%

🚨 Necrotising Fasciitis

Recognition

Pain disproportionate to appearance, then dusky/grey skin colour changes, blistering, necrosis. Systemic sepsis. Crepitus (subcutaneous gas) if present is diagnostic.

Critical Actions

  • SURGICAL EMERGENCY — call 112/999/911
  • Do NOT delay surgery for imaging
  • Broad-spectrum IV antibiotics immediately (carbapenem + clindamycin)
  • HDU/ICU, aggressive resuscitation
  • IVIG for Group A Streptococcal type II
  • Mortality 20-30%

🚨 DRESS Syndrome

Recognition

2-8 weeks after starting new drug: widespread rash + fever + lymphadenopathy + organ involvement (hepatitis most common). Eosinophilia + atypical lymphocytes.

Common Culprit Drugs

  • Aromatic anticonvulsants: phenytoin, carbamazepine, phenobarbitone
  • Allopurinol, dapsone, minocycline, sulfonamides, abacavir

Critical Actions

  • STOP the causative drug immediately
  • Hospital admission for daily organ monitoring (LFTs, renal function)
  • Systemic corticosteroids in most cases (prednisolone 0.5-1mg/kg/day)
  • RegiSCAR scoring criteria for diagnosis

Generalised Erythroderma

90%+ BSA erythema and scaling. Causes: psoriasis flare (most common), atopic eczema flare, drug reaction, CTCL/Sezary syndrome, pityriasis rubra pilaris. Complications: thermoregulatory failure, high-output cardiac failure, hypoalbuminaemia, fluid/electrolyte disturbance, secondary infection. Hospital admission, treat underlying cause, emollients, temperature regulation, cardiac monitoring, nutritional support.

Skin of Colour

Dedicated dermatology guidance for Fitzpatrick skin types IV-VI — addressing under-representation in clinical training and the different presentations of skin disease in darker skin tones.

🧹
Skin of colour (Fitzpatrick IV-VI) represents the majority of the global population but has historically been under-represented in dermatology training. Clinical presentations differ significantly from lighter skin tones — this section addresses that gap.

Key Differences and Considerations

Post-Inflammatory Hyperpigmentation (PIH)

Extremely common, often more distressing than the primary skin condition. Any skin inflammation leaves dark brown-grey macules persisting months-years (eczema, acne, psoriasis, insect bites, injuries). Prevention is key. Management: topical retinoids, azelaic acid, niacinamide, vitamin C, tranexamic acid (topical/oral), hydroquinone short courses, SPF 50+ DAILY (mandatory). Caution with lasers and peels — paradoxical worsening of PIH possible.

Keloids and Hypertrophic Scars

Keloids extend BEYOND the original wound boundary. Genetic predisposition. 5-15x more prevalent in Fitzpatrick V-VI. Common sites: earlobes, chest, upper back, jaw. Prevention most important — avoid unnecessary surgery/piercings in high-risk individuals. Intralesional triamcinolone every 4-6 weeks, silicone gel sheeting, surgical excision + immediate post-op radiotherapy for refractory cases.

Eczema in Skin of Colour

Erythema appears dark brown, grey, or purplish rather than red — leading to under-diagnosis and under-treatment. More commonly: follicular accentuation (gooseflesh-like), lichenification, and PIH. DLQI impact often higher due to pigmentation burden. SCORAD/EASI may underestimate severity.

Psoriasis in Skin of Colour

Plaques appear darker (brown/violaceous not red) with less visible scale on darker skin. PASI scores may underestimate severity (erythema component underscored). Post-treatment hypopigmentation (white patches where plaques resolved) can be very distressing. Guttate psoriasis may be difficult to distinguish from pityriasis rosea.

Melasma (predominantly Fitzpatrick III-V)

Symmetrical brown-grey facial hyperpigmentation. Exacerbated by UV, hormonal factors (pregnancy, COCP, HRT). Strict SPF 50+ (most important), hydroquinone 2-4% (short courses), triple Kligman combination, azelaic acid, tranexamic acid oral/topical (strong emerging evidence), niacinamide. Chemical peels and lasers: use with caution and expertise.

Acne Keloidalis Nuchae (AKN)

Follicular papules/pustules at nape/occipital scalp progressing to keloidal scarring. Predominantly affects Black men and those of African descent. Avoid close shaving/haircuts. Topical steroids, retinoids, intralesional steroids, antibiotics, laser epilation for severe cases.

Hair Disorders in Afro-Caribbean Hair

Traction alopecia: most common cause of hair loss in Black women (tight braids, weaves, extensions, relaxers). Central centrifugal cicatricial alopecia (CCCA): progressive scarring alopecia at crown, predominantly Black women. Pseudofolliculitis barbae: ingrown hairs from shaving — very common in Black men, can lead to PIH and scarring. Dermatology-aware haircare counselling essential.
